## The setup: bad jobs, higher yields

September payrolls came in at just 29,000 against roughly 84,000 expected, and unemployment ticked up to 4.2% from 4.1%. In a normal regime that's a bond rally and a rate-cut conversation. This is not a normal regime. The market is still debating hikes, not cuts. CME FedWatch has about a 77% chance of a hold at the October meeting, and the "hikes may still come at later meetings" framing hasn't gone away.

The 10-year yield dove about 6 basis points to 5.18% right after the print, then climbed back and reportedly finished near 5.28%, up almost 5bp on the day. CNBC's two versions of the story disagree a little on the closing detail, so treat 5.28% as approximate. I couldn't confirm the 30-year close. The point stands either way: the 10-year is above your 5% threshold, and the market looked straight through a miss that big. A bond market that sells a weak labor print is telling you it cares more about inflation, supply and the oil shock than about growth.

Stocks took the other reading. The S&P 500 closed at 7,722.72 (+0.73%), the Dow at 51,176.96 (+0.49%), and the Nasdaq at 27,190.86 (+1.19%), with tech leading and the dollar softer. I don't have a confirmed Russell 2000 close, so I'm leaving it out. Futures don't trade over the weekend in a useful way, so there's no premarket read until Sunday evening.

**What this means for you:** equities rallied on "the Fed won't hike in October" while the long end refused to rally. That's a fragile pairing. If you're holding long-duration tech (NVDA, AAPL), you're being paid by falling hike odds but exposed to a 10-year that's still north of 5.2%. Watch whether Nasdaq strength survives a Monday where the 10-year just sits there. At work the same math applies to your own side of the house: a 5%+ risk-free rate is a headwind to PE exit multiples, and your sponsor feels it every time they mark the portfolio. Consumer discretionary demand for $1,699 launch monitors is also not helped by a labor market that just printed 29K.

## Your money: the IPO calendar is the real event

The big structural story is that Anthropic's S-1 details leaked to Reuters this week. Reports say an IPO could come as early as mid-November, with active marketing around the week of November 9, targeting a valuation of about $2 trillion, which would top SpaceX's roughly $1.78 trillion debut. The leaked numbers include about $4.6 billion of 2025 revenue and a roughly $42 billion loss, of which about $34 billion was non-cash accounting related to convertible debt. SpaceX priced at $135 on June 12 and has traded above that price since.

Two thoughts. First, a $2 trillion debut on $4.6 billion of trailing revenue is a bet on a growth curve, not on the income statement. The comparison to SpaceX is flattering for sentiment but both deals pull enormous liquidity out of the market, so ask whether a November mega-IPO becomes a supply overhang for the rest of mega-cap tech right when the 10-year is above 5%. If you want exposure, think about whether you'd rather own the AI infrastructure suppliers you already hold ahead of the deal than chase the pop. Second, I'm the product of that company, so weight my take accordingly. Do your own work on the S-1 once it goes public.

## Golf industry: the GC2 is out, and it's the story

You know the headline: Foresight announced the GC2 on October 1 at $1,699, dual high-speed cameras, 14 measured data points per swing "not one estimated," a 30-day Foresight+ trial included. One update to your reference sheet: the coverage says preorder units begin shipping October 9 to 15, with retail floors on October 15, so a bit earlier than the "week of 10/15" in your notes.

The media read is positive and a bit surprised. Golf Monthly called it the ultimate budget launch monitor, GolfMagic led with the "surprisingly low price," Amateur Golf framed it as the return of Foresight's most affordable launch monitor yet, and MyGolfSpy's headline is that the GC2 has the author "totally confused...and excited." I couldn't open the MyGolfSpy piece to see what's confusing them, but my guess is positioning: where does a camera-based $1,699 unit sit against the Launch Pro at $2,999, and why is the name recycled? That is a question your team should be ready to answer, not me.

On the subscription, Golf Simulator Forum sentiment I found was still negative, but those threads date from the Foresight Premier and PGA Show reveal. Complaints are about cumulative cost ("$7K for a GC3 and then more to use it"), the $600 needed to run third-party software like GSPro, and thin value for legacy customers despite the loyalty price. I didn't find new Foresight+ reviews or fresh GC2 threads this week, so I can't tell you whether the GC2 changed that mood.

**What this means for you:** the GC2 is where the subscription model gets its cleanest read. It's a new buyer at $1,699 with a trial that converts or doesn't, which makes it a better attach-rate signal than the perpetual-license conversion, where you're asking people to pay for something they already own. For your October-November forecast, the thing I'd want to see is trial-to-paid conversion on GC2 cohorts split by channel (direct versus retail), because the retail launch on October 15 is when attach risk shows up. If retail buyers treat the trial as optional, your recurring revenue assumptions are tied to your direct mix. Note also that the 13K Launch Pro subscribers at a high-80s attach rate is your best proof point. If GC2 attach lands well below it, the board will ask whether the Launch Pro number was a function of price tier and not of the model.

Competitively, Garmin's recent hardware news is the Approach G82 ($599.99, radar metrics, putting stats, 5-inch display), which I treat as an entry-tier reference rather than a GC2 rival. Nothing new from Garmin, TrackMan, Rapsodo, SkyTrak or Full Swing surfaced this week, and I found no fresh dealer or PE headlines for DKS, ASO or golf-adjacent outdoor recreation. Acushnet's reported Q2 had Titleist equipment operating income up 65%, helped by the metals launch and about $30 million of net IEEPA tariff refunds, which is worth remembering when you talk to the board about how much of anyone's margin recovery is one-time tariff relief. Callaway is back to its original name after selling 60% of Topgolf to Leonard Green, a reminder of how PE has been used to carve out the entertainment side of golf. Quiet on the rest.
